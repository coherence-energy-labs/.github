Assay

Green means nothing until red is possible

Every regulated industry runs on checks that nobody has ever proven can fail. We attack the check, not the claim — and we publish what survives, what broke, and what we could not establish. We will find a control that does nothing in every system we are pointed at. That is a testable claim, and the first system we tested it on was our own.

The cold open

A test that cannot go red gets trusted. That is worse than a test that fails.

In August 2026 a GPU guard in this estate measured peer VRAM instead of free VRAM. Seven tests silently skipped. Five real cross-repository failures stayed invisible for an unknown length of time — because a skip reads exactly like a pass.

Separately, an SMP kernel reported LOCKED 800 EXPECT 800 as proof a spinlock held. The same kernel compiled without the lock produced the identical total. The check was satisfied by the emulator and proved nothing.

Separately again, a production solver shipped with a default iteration count and a docstring promising convergence. Measured against a closed-form Green's function it was 44.53% wrong. Every test it had compared the solver to itself, to a snapshot, or to a downstream score. None of them could see a solver converging to the wrong answer.

These are not three bugs. They are one failure class, and it is the failure class that every audit, every compliance portal, every SOC 2 report, and every model-validation PDF on earth is structurally unable to detect — because they were all built to answer "does evidence exist?" rather than "could this evidence-producing system detect a lie?"

The world checks whether the box was ticked.
Nobody checks whether the box could have failed. That gap is the company.

Moat

Incumbents are structurally barred from our best output

The question every investor asks: why won't a Big Four assurance practice, or an established MRV vendor, simply build this?

Not because they lack engineers. Because their revenue is issuing opinions, and our best product tells a client that the opinion they just bought was protecting nothing. An assurance firm that ships DARK CONTROL is competing with itself. That is a structural exclusion, not a cultural one, and it does not resolve with budget.

The second moat compounds. A traditional compliance database gets bigger as customers join. An attack corpus gets better at attacking everyone. Customer A exposes a calibration certificate that stays cryptographically valid past its authorized measurement window; we generalize it, calibrate it, prove it kills the vulnerable fixture and spares the good twin, and release it. Customers B through E are attacked by it that night — and every previously issued assurance object whose scope intersects it flips to NEW APPLICABLE ATTACK — RE-ASSESSMENT REQUIRED.

More customers → more failure modes → stronger corpus → more valuable assurance. And the corpus is worth more than the code, because the expensive part of an attack is never writing it. It is proving it fires and does not cry wolf — which is exactly the thing we measured ourselves failing at this morning, and fixed.

What we are not

Assay is never the authority

We do not replace the FDA. We do not determine EU legal compliance. We do not replace an accredited CBAM verifier — that verification is performed by accredited parties and will stay that way.

We are the technical evidence substrate underneath those decisions. An auditor's opinion sits in the graph. An accredited signature sits in the graph. A clinician's judgment sits in the graph. What Assay will never do is silently upgrade professional judgment into mathematical proof. That restraint is why a regulated buyer can adopt us at all.

Risks we are not hiding

Our output is an accusation. A false DARK CONTROL is a reputational and possibly legal event. We measured a 46–69% false-positive rate in one of our own detectors this morning. The answer is not to promise it won't happen — it is that every attack ships a measured FP rate against a real corpus, and the corpus keeps its own graveyard of attacks that were wrong.

Default private. Public arena limited to our own projects, opt-in open source, and explicit challenges. We will not ship "paste any company's repo and we'll tell the world what's broken."

Language stays mechanical. Never "Company X lied." Always "gate G remained green after mutation M removed condition P." Facts, artifacts, scope.

Novelty unproven. Property testing, mutation testing, fuzzing, chaos engineering, formal verification and adversarial ML each hold pieces. What is plausibly unclaimed is the composition. We will research that before we market it — and in the ZK lane we have named competitors and can measure the claim instead of asserting it.
